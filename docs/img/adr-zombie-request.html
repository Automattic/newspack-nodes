A worker is an HTTP request that answers before it runs

The platform caps a request at 15 minutes and offers no resident process. So the endpoint admits the spawn, sends its 200 and finishes the request, closing the connection, and the process that served it goes on as the worker for ~595 seconds, until it releases its lock and POSTs its own successor.

Rejected: holding the connection for the worker's life

No caller waits, but the platform does: nginx counts a site's connections to itself, five at most (selfdos), and holds a spawn's until the worker returns or 120 s pass. A respawn wave then refuses every other self-request. The finish runs in rest_pre_serve_request, once WordPress has set the headers, never ahead of the WP_REST_Response.

Rejected: a resident daemon, or rescue-only restart

A daemon is unavailable on the platform. Without self-respawn, every ~10-minute recycle would idle the slot until a peer's stale-lock rescue; self-respawn hands off immediately and leaves the peer scan as the safety net (ADR-9), not the scheduler.

What it costs

Correctness rests on flawless offsetlog resume across ~144 respawns a day per worker, and on release-before-respawn. Reversed, the successor's acquire hits the still-held lock and skips. Revisit if the platform lifts the time cap or offers resident workers: the dance collapses into a normal long-lived loop.
